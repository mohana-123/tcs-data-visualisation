Data Visualisation: Framing the Business Problem

The focus was on structuring business-driven analytical questions aligned with leadership goals.

The scenario required viewing revenue performance from both strategic (CEO) and customer/marketing (CMO) perspectives.

CEO-focused analysis themes:

1. In which region are your products are generating more revenue?
2. Do you observe any seasonal trends for your products?
3. Are our revenue dependent on the top customers or else they diversified?
4. which months generate the lowest revenue? and why? 

CMO-focused analysis themes:

1. what is the average duration taking for a customer to buy again any of the product?
2. Who are the top 20% customers revenue and what are the top 5 products they are buying frequently?
3. what is a top region that customers buying your any of your products?
4. are there are any conditions that affecting the sales despite of effective promotions of some low monetary products during some timelines?

This exercise reinforced the importance of problem framing, stakeholder alignment, and metric selection.

Today I deep-dived into two important data visualization concepts: Grammar of Graphics and choosing the right chart for the right question.
One key learning: visualization is not about picking random charts -- it’s about mapping data systematically.
Grammar of Graphics helped me understand that every chart is built from components:
Data -> Aesthetics -> Geometry -> Scales -> Facets.
It made me realize that good visuals are designed, not just created.
The second concept reinforced something even more practical:
Always choose a chart based on the business question.
=> Comparing categories? -> Bar chart
=> Trends over time? -> Line chart
=> Distribution? -> Histogram
=> Relationship? -> Scatter plot
Clear question -> Correct visual -> Clear insight.

It’s not about making them attractive - it’s about making them meaningful.

Next, I conducted data quality checks.
I identified null customer IDs, negative quantities, zero pricing issues, and cancelled transactions. Cleaning these inconsistencies was critical before calculating revenue or customer metrics.

If I were presenting to a CMO, these are the 4 questions I would expect:
1. How long does it take customers to purchase again?
 Using SQL window functions and DAX, I calculated the average repurchase cycle.
 Result: Customers return every 44 days on average.
 This directly informs remarketing timing and retention campaigns.
so we must trigger the marketing campaign and notification after 30 days
2. Are we dependent on a small group of customers?
 I performed a Pareto analysis 
 The cumulative revenue curve clearly shows revenue concentration - a small percentage of customers drive a large share of total revenue.
 This highlights both loyalty strength and concentration risk. 
3. Which region generates the most revenue?
 From the country-level analysis
 Netherlands and EIRE lead revenue contribution, followed by Germany and France.
 This indicates where expansion focus or localized campaigns would be most effective. but the top 2 have a concentration risk the revenue must be diversified to more countries
4. Are promotions and low-price products actually increasing revenue?
 From the monthly trend, revenue and quantity rise sharply from September to November, peaking in November.
 However, early-year dips suggest seasonality impacts sales more than pricing strategy alone.
Key insight:
 Customer behavior and geographic concentration matter more than raw sales volume.
 Marketing strategy should focus on high-value customer retention and regional optimization.
